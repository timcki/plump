The bottom rowFive bars at panel scale, 480×64, with a hint of the content above them. What is off about the one that ships is not the icons: it is that the active tab is dressed as a cursor, and that the bar has no edge.

  1. The active tab wears the cursor's clothes. A filled rounded rectangle with a knocked-out glyph is exactly how a selected row is drawn — and the tab bar has no cursor. Left and Right switch tabs only when the screen declines them; you can never move onto the bar. On Home you now see two inversions at once, one meaning “the cursor is here” and one meaning “you are on this screen”.
  2. It is the heaviest mark on the display. 52×52 of solid ink, blacker and larger than the card's 2 px border, spent on the only element that is not a press target.
  3. It has no edge. TabBar::draw clears the bar to paper and puts icons on it — that is all. Every mockup since the first drew a hairline above it; the device never has. The top status bar has the same hole.
  4. It is the only unlabelled control on the device. Settings rows, sheet hints, section captions, the sleep card: everything else says its name. The bar asks you to recognise five glyphs.
  5. Its corner radius matches nothing. 4 px, where row groups are 6, the card is 6 and a sheet is 10.

As it ships

TodayThe filled square, the missing rule, and four glyphs with nothing to say. The bar reads as a control you could operate and cannot.

A · Outlined, ruledThree lines of change: the square becomes an outline at the group radius, and the hairline arrives. The filled treatment goes back to meaning only one thing. Still an outlined box around an icon, which is the settings chip's job, and still five silent glyphs.

B · RailedNo box at all. A five-segment rail along the bottom edge, the active segment filled and the rest hairline: which of five, said with the same tube the reader footer and every position bar already use. The active icon steps up one rasterisation tier. Nothing on the bar can be mistaken for something you press.

Only the active slot is named — five words would be a menu, one word is a label. It costs 13 px of the bar's 64 and answers all five complaints at once.

  1. The count goes in every option. “4 books” counts four rows that are on screen and countable. It was the one piece of text on Home that told you something you could already see.
  2. Why the rule over the word. Two objects with different borders, separated by a gap, do not need to be told apart in words. What the gap alone cannot say is that a new kind of thing begins — a short centred rule says exactly that, in a mark the reader's loading plate already uses, and it never needs updating.
  3. Why not the full-width rule. The row group's own outline is a full-width line 6 px below it. Two parallel horizontal lines that close to each other read as a mistake; the short centred rule does not compete with an edge.
  4. Where the bar and the separator meet. Both redesigns lean on the same idea: on a screen this small, a hairline or a segment says what a filled shape or a label was being used to say, and costs a fraction of the ink. The tab bar's rail and the separator's rule are the same gesture at two scales.
  5. One thing the bar cannot fix. Naming only the active tab means the other four stay glyphs. That is the right trade at 96 px a slot — five tracked words across 480 px would have to shrink below the chrome font to fit — but it does mean the icons still have to be learned once.
